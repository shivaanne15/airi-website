Short Bowel Syndrome

Understanding your remaining intestinal anatomy is an important part of managing short bowel syndrome and developing an individualized nutrition and treatment plan.

What is short bowel syndrome?

Short bowel syndrome (SBS) occurs when the small intestine cannot absorb enough fluids and nutrients to meet the body's needs, usually because part of the intestine has been surgically removed or is not functioning properly.

The amount and location of the remaining intestine including whether the colon remains connected influence nutrient and fluid absorption, treatment needs, and the potential to reduce dependence on parenteral nutrition over time.

Why intestinal anatomy matters

Short bowel syndrome affects people differently depending on how much intestine remains, which sections remain, and whether the colon is still connected. Understanding your anatomy helps your care team individualize nutrition, hydration, medications, and other treatments.

Stomach

The stomach begins breaking down food before it enters the small intestine. After significant bowel resection, some people with SBS may produce excess stomach acid, which can increase intestinal output and affect digestion.

Jejunum

The jejunum absorbs many nutrients, fluids, and minerals. When part of the jejunum is removed, the remaining intestine may adapt over time and take on some of its absorptive function.

Ileum

The ileum plays an important role in absorbing nutrients, including vitamin B12 and bile acids. Loss of the ileum can affect nutrient and fat absorption and may require additional nutrition support or supplementation.

Colon

When the colon remains connected, it can help absorb additional fluids and electrolytes and recover energy from nutrients that were not absorbed by the small intestine.

What causes short bowel syndrome?

Short bowel syndrome most often develops after a significant portion of the small intestine has been surgically removed. Conditions that may lead to SBS include:

  • Crohn's disease and other severe intestinal disease
  • Loss of blood flow to the intestine, including mesenteric ischemia
  • Volvulus or other intestinal obstruction
  • Complications following abdominal or intestinal surgery
  • Certain cancers or radiation injury
  • Abdominal trauma

In some cases, severe intestinal dysfunction can cause similar problems with nutrient and fluid absorption even when the intestine has not been surgically removed.

Signs and symptoms of short bowel syndrome

Symptoms vary depending on how much intestine remains and which sections are affected. Common symptoms may include:

  • Frequent diarrhea or high ostomy output
  • Dehydration and excessive thirst
  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Fatigue or weakness
  • Abdominal cramping or bloating
  • Vitamin, mineral, or electrolyte deficiencies

How is short bowel syndrome diagnosed?

Short bowel syndrome is diagnosed based on your medical and surgical history, symptoms, remaining intestinal anatomy, and nutritional status. Your care team may use:

  • Medical history and physical exam — to understand your intestinal surgery, symptoms, weight changes, and signs of malnutrition or dehydration
  • Blood tests — to check electrolytes, vitamin and mineral levels, blood counts, and liver and kidney function
  • Imaging — such as X-rays or CT scans when needed to evaluate your remaining intestine or look for complications
  • Additional testing — stool tests, endoscopy, or other studies may be used depending on your symptoms and medical history

How short bowel syndrome is managed

Treatment is individualized based on your remaining intestinal anatomy, symptoms, nutrition and hydration needs, and how well your intestine adapts over time. The goals are to:

  • Maintain adequate nutrition and hydration
  • Prevent and manage complications
  • Improve the intestine's ability to absorb nutrients and fluids
  • Reduce dependence on parenteral nutrition and IV fluids when possible

Intestinal rehabilitation may include changes to diet and fluid intake, medications to manage symptoms and improve absorption, nutrition support, and ongoing monitoring. Your care plan may change over time as your intestine adapts and your needs change.

Complications of short bowel syndrome

Short bowel syndrome can affect hydration, nutrition, and other areas of your health. Your care team will monitor for complications and adjust your treatment based on your symptoms, intestinal anatomy, laboratory results, and nutrition needs.

Dehydration and electrolyte imbalances

Diarrhea or high ostomy output can cause significant fluid and electrolyte losses. Your care team may monitor sodium, potassium, magnesium, kidney function, and other measures of hydration.

Vitamin and mineral deficiencies

Reduced intestinal absorption can lead to deficiencies in vitamins and minerals, including vitamin B12 and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K. Your needs depend on which parts of the intestine remain.

Digestive complications

Some people with SBS experience small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), excess stomach acid, fat malabsorption, or increased stool or ostomy output. Treatment is individualized based on the cause and symptoms.

Kidney and bone health

SBS can increase the risk of kidney stones and metabolic bone disease. Ongoing monitoring may include kidney function, vitamin and mineral levels, and bone health.

Parenteral nutrition and central-line complications

Patients who require long-term parenteral nutrition may need monitoring for central-line infections, blood clots, liver complications, and other issues related to ongoing nutrition support.

Metabolic complications

Changes in intestinal absorption can occasionally contribute to acid-base or other metabolic problems. Your care team may use laboratory testing and symptoms to identify these complications.

Know when to seek help. Contact your care team for significant changes in diarrhea or ostomy output, difficulty staying hydrated, reduced urination, worsening weakness, or other new or concerning symptoms. Seek urgent medical care for severe symptoms.
